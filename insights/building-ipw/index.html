BUILDING IPW · SEPTEMBER 24, 2026 · BY LARRY CURETON

One leader.
A connected delivery chain.

Building Infrastructure Product Works drew on product management, architecture, engineering, cloud, delivery and leadership together. That breadth made it possible to carry decisions from the original problem into working, reviewable software.

This article concerns the work of building IPW. Staffing to deploy, operate, support or govern it is a separate question.

END-TO-END PLATFORM PRODUCT LEADERSHIP

Connect the decisions across disciplines.

An End-to-End Platform Product Leader can connect the customer problem, product roadmap, architecture, implementation and delivery evidence. The value comes from understanding how a decision in one area affects the others.

In building IPW, that meant drawing on several kinds of knowledge:

  • Product management and roadmaps: define the problem, prioritize outcomes and sequence the work.
  • Architecture and cloud: establish product boundaries, reusable patterns and technical constraints.
  • Engineering, GitHub and Visual Studio: connect implementation with source control, tests and reviewable changes.
  • Jira, Azure DevOps and project management: understand how requirements, dependencies and delivery work stay traceable.
  • Leadership: make tradeoffs, maintain direction and retain responsibility for the result.

These are contributing areas of expertise, not a claim that every named tool is part of IPW's runtime. AI-assisted implementation can extend the builder's capacity; judgment, acceptance and accountability still need an owner.

WHERE CAPACITY COULD BE RECOVERED

Fewer handoffs. More time for delivery.

When development responsibilities are split across roles, a change may require several rounds of explanation, coordination and document reconciliation. A leader who understands the whole chain can reduce some of that work by maintaining shared context from intent through implementation.

This is a possible benefit of integrated leadership in a role-separated delivery model. It is not a finding about the performance or staffing of any particular employer, government organization or team.

ILLUSTRATIVE DEVELOPMENT SCENARIO

12.5% of development-team capacity recovered

Assume an eight-person team spends 25% of its total working time on coordination and handoffs. If it reduces that effort by 50%, while maintaining delivery quality and necessary controls:

25% × 50% = 12.5% of total team capacity.
8 people × 12.5% = the equivalent of one full-time person's working hours over the same period.

Both percentages are assumptions chosen to explain the calculation. They are not industry benchmarks, measured IPW results or a forecast of savings.

The recovered time could go toward implementation, testing, documentation or additional demand. Turning capacity into fewer positions would require separate evidence about workload, skills, coverage and service obligations. The example is not a staffing recommendation.

BUILDING AND OPERATING ARE DIFFERENT RESPONSIBILITIES

A lean build does not establish a lean operating model.

The estimate above does not cover deployment, production operations, user support, security monitoring, independent review or governance. Those responsibilities need staffing appropriate to the service. Knowledge must also be documented and transferable so continuity does not depend on one person.

WHAT WOULD MAKE THE ESTIMATE EVIDENCE?

Measure comparable work before claiming savings.

A useful evaluation would record coordination hours, delivery time, accepted scope, defects and rework before and after a change. It would also account for AI and tooling costs, specialist contributions and review effort. Equivalent outcomes and preserved controls matter more than a smaller headcount.

IPW provides an independent example of connected product construction. It is not a controlled staffing study or production-scale proof of the percentage shown here.
